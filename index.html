Shuxun Tian 田树旬

I am a lecturer and M.Sc. supervisor in the Department of Astronomy at Beijing Normal University. I earned my Ph.D. in theoretical physics from Wuhan University in 2020, then worked as a postdoctoral researcher at Beijing Normal University, and joined the faculty in 2024. My research focuses on developing theories of gravity and exploring their applications in cosmology and astrophysics. I proposed gravitational caloric theory and a microlensing method for testing gravity.

Research Highlights

2026

Gravitational Caloric Theory

S. X. Tian, Gravitational caloric theory: From early dark energy to a wide variety of gravitational phenomena, Phys. Rev. D 114, 044042 (2026). arXiv:2608.07318.

S. X. Tian and Z.-H. Zhu, Black holes with conical infinity in gravitational caloric theory, submitted to Phys. Rev. D (2026). arXiv:2610.09606.

Gravitational caloric theory generalizes general relativity from the fluid side. Its initial motivation was to use the cosmic radiation-matter transition to trigger early dark energy and resolve the associated coincidence problem. Beyond this, the theory has a broad range of cosmological and astrophysical applications, including the early static hot Universe, conical black holes, and gravitational-wave physics.

The early static hot Universe and conical black holes may have particular astronomical relevance. The former faces theoretical challenges and will be discussed separately. Conical black holes in gravitational caloric theory can remain arbitrarily close to Schwarzschild in the strong-field (near-horizon) and weak-field (e.g., Solar System) regimes, yet approach conical geometry in the extremely weak-field regime. This highlights the importance of testing gravity theories in this regime.

Observational Tests of Gravity Theories

X. Jin, S. Cao, X. Chen, Y. Nan, X. Tai, and S. Tian, Testing general relativity with binary lens microlensing events, Astrophys. J. Lett. 1008, L41 (2026). arXiv:2610.11949.

Alternatives to general relativity may predict different properties of local gravitational systems. We explore how observations of individual sources with existing or near-future facilities can test these predictions, focusing on regimes that remain poorly constrained. Our current focus is the extremely weak-field regime, motivated in part by conical black holes in gravitational caloric theory. These solutions illustrate how close agreement with general relativity in stronger fields can coexist with significant deviations in extremely weak fields. We hope to identify observational probes that could reveal such new physics, although we have not yet found a sufficiently compelling one. Nevertheless, we have proposed a binary microlensing method for testing gravity as a step in this direction.
